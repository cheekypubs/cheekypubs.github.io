Clarissa Cottonmoon was looking at the clock. As the director of the Birchbright Theater, she was used to things running on time. It, not her, she thought sometimes, was the true theater director. There was so much to do, and her only currency for doing it was scant, unmerciful time. But now 37 minutes were gone, times *two*. She was about to call one of them when she heard a door open in the lobby. When she went out, her assistants were there: Corey and Chelsea. They were shaking off the rain. Corey smiled as Clarissa came out of her office, but Chelsea looked a bit nervous.

“Um…,” was Corey’s reply. Clarissa could see his mind working, trying to come up with a reason why they couldn’t. But he only replied, “definitely,” and smiled again. And Chelsea smiled too.

Even though Corey was a theater kid, he had the face of a quarterback on a CW show: summer-blue eyes and artfully careless hair, jawline like a Berkshire ridge. When Clarissa had interviewed him for the job, she had immediately clocked that he was used to getting away with things. But his love for the theater was sincere; he had been coming to performances since he was a little kid. He had even performed here, though as Corey got older it became clear he was more suited to the work behind the stage than on it. And so he had gotten involved in tech crews, and had taken to it quickly. Corey’s father was a contractor in town and had taught him some basic construction techniques. He brought a lot to the local theater community and seemed serious, and so Clarissa had hired him for the internship. She hoped it would not prove to be a mistake.

“No, thanks Chelsea, let’s just get to work. I’d like to get the initial background painting done today, I want it to be finished and dry for Anton to draft the detailing on Thursday. From here, they diverged, each moving to their respective tasks. Clarissa disappeared into the scene shop. By the time she looked up again, it was noon.

She strolled out of the shop, intending to check on her tardy charges, when she spied someone slipping around the curtain onto the stage. Startled, she rushed after them. It was only a dozen steps to the heavy red curtain, and she pushed through and was blinded. The stage lights were all on full blast. Clarissa could feel the heat from them, a bright hose of warm yellow light. It took her a second to adjust her eyes, and by the time she did the theater was empty. But she hadn’t heard anyone leave. She stood blinking on the stage, confused, trying to figure out what had just happened. But then she noticed something else: the background that had been propped up on stage was still there. And it was bleeding.

Clarissa gasped. But then she frowned and stepped closer. It wasn’t bleeding: it was leaking red paint. And then Clarissa gasped again: Corey had done the requested painting, but somehow the background had been left under the lights, and all the paint had melted. Reds and blues ran into each other; much of what ought to have been a townscape was now a mottled, copperish brown. There was no way to paint over this: it was ruined. The whole slat would have to be recut and redone. Clarissa marched out of the theater. Corey, she thought, or whoever had left these lights on, had a lot of explaining to do.

It took fifteen minutes to find Corey. He wasn’t in the lobby, or the men’s room, or outside the front door. Clarissa was passing the coat check when she heard a noise. She moved quickly to the lobby window and peered in. At first it looked empty, nothing but a quiet thicket of empty hangers. But then she looked down. “Um, hi Clarissa.”

“I need both of you to meet me in my office in five minutes. Before that, you will both go onstage and look at the set piece that’s there.”

With that, Clarissa turned and walked away. Behind her, she heard Chelsea whimper.
